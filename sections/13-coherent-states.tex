\subsection{Summary of quadrature operators}

\hl{A coherent state is a linear superposition of number states} and this provides the closest approximation of a radiation by a laser. 

We already learned the relationship between the quadrature operators and the creation and annihilation operators:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-quadratures-creation-annihilation-operators}
\begin{cases}
    \hat{x}_1 = \frac{1}{2}\, (\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger ) \\
    \hat{x}_2 = \frac{i}{2}\, (\hat{a}-\hat{a}^\dagger ) 
\end{cases} \iff \hspace{2ex} \begin{cases}
    \hat{a} \hspace{0.23cm} = \hat{x}_1+i\hat{x}_2\\
    \hat{a}^\dagger  = \hat{x}_1-i\hat{x}_2
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

We are then allowed to write the Hamiltonian in terms of the quadrature operators:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-hamiltonian-quadratures}
\hat{H} = \hbar \omega \left(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}+\frac{1}{2}\right) =
\hbar \omega \left(\hat{x}_1^2+\hat{x}_2^2\right).
\end{equation}

We want to rely on photon number state, the basis can be used for black body radiation and chaotic light too. Remember that we have:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-number-operator-eigenvalue-equation}
\hat{n}\,\ket{n} = n\, \ket{n},
\end{equation}

with uncertainty
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-number-operator-uncertainty}
\begin{split}
\braket{\Delta n^2} &= \braket{\hat{n}^2} - \braket{\hat{n}}^2 =\\[0.5ex]
&= \braket{n|\hat{n}\, \hat{n}|n}-(\braket{n|\hat{n}|n})^2 = \\[0.5ex]
&= n^2-n^2 = 0
\end{split}
\end{equation}

and we can write the Hamiltonian eigenvalue equation both in terms of the number operator and the quadrature operators:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-hamiltonian-eigenvalue-equation}
\hat{H}\, \ket{n} = \hbar \omega \left(\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}+\frac{1}{2} \right)\, \ket{n} =\hbar \omega \bigl(n+\frac{1}{2}\bigr) = \hbar \omega \left(\hat{x}_1^2+\hat{x}_2^2\right)\, \ket{n}
\end{equation}

simplifying:
\begin{equation}
\left(\hat{x}_1^2+\hat{x}_2^2\right)\, \ket{n} = \left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right) \, \ket{n}
\end{equation}
\vspace{1ex}

A crucial property of any number state $\ket{n}$ is that the expectation values of the standard quadratures vanish: $\braket{n|\hat{x}_1|n} = \braket{n|\hat{x}_2|n} = 0$. Consequently, if we define a generalized rotated quadrature operator as:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-rotated-quadrature-operator}
X_\theta = \cos(\theta)\, \hat{x}_1+\sin(\theta) \, \hat{x}_2
\end{equation}

its expectation value remains zero ($\braket{n|X_\theta|n} = 0$) for any phase angle $\theta$. This mathematically implies that the average electric field of a Fock state is identically zero, meaning its classical phase is completely undefined and random.

This highlights a fundamental quantum complementarity: \hl{a Fock state possesses a perfectly sharp energy and photon number ($\braket{\Delta n^2} = 0$), but lacks any classical phase analogue}. Conversely, a state with a well-defined classical phase must exhibit a broad photon number distribution, obeying a fundamental uncertainty relation similar to position and momentum.

\hl{The vacuum state $\ket{0}$ acts as the effective ground state of the system when thermal fluctuations are negligible compared to the quantum excitation energy}, namely when:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-vacuum-thermal-condition}
k_B T \ll \hbar \omega.
\end{equation}

While the single-photon state $\ket{1}$ can be generated experimentally via atomic cascade emissions or through spontaneous parametric down-conversion (SPDC), preparing higher excited Fock states ($\ket{2}$, $\ket{3}$, $\dots$) becomes increasingly challenging in the laboratory. 

\calloutinfo{%
Because Fock states are highly non-classical, macroscopically fragile, and lack a defined phase, they cannot describe the continuous, stable wave emitted by a standard laser. This limitation strongly motivates the introduction of \textbf{coherent states}, which balance number and phase uncertainties to properly mimic classical radiation.
}

\subsection{Coherent States Formalism}

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Displacement operator}

A single-mode laser light operating well above threshold possesses a well-defined, stable phase. Such classical-like radiation cannot be described by a single Fock state, but corresponds to a specific linear superposition known as a \textbf{coherent state}, denoted as $\ket{\alpha}$.

Classically, we can visualize a mode being excited by an external source that shifts the electric field from zero to a finite classical amplitude $\vec{E} \propto |\alpha| \neq 0$, which in turn drives the magnetic field, generating a self-propagating harmonic wave. In the quantum framework, this process is modeled by displacing the vacuum state away from the origin of the phase space to induce an oscillating expectation value for the field.

To formalize this, we introduce the \hl{unitary \textbf{displacement operator} $\hat{D}(\alpha)$}, which shifts the vacuum state by a complex amplitude $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-displacement-operator-definition}
\ket{\alpha} = \hat{D}(\alpha) \ket{0}.
\end{equation}

The displacement operator features the exponentiation of the ladder operators:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-displacement-operator-exponential}
\hat{D}(\alpha) = e^{\alpha \hat{a}^\dagger  - \alpha^\star \hat{a}}.
\end{equation}

From its definition and the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff lemma, $\hat{D}(\alpha)$ satisfies the following fundamental properties:
\begin{align}
\Rnum{1}&.\hspace{1ex} \hat{D}^\dagger (\alpha) = \hat{D}^{-1}(\alpha) = \hat{D}(-\alpha), \label{eqn:13-displacement-property-1} \\
\Rnum{2}&.\hspace{1ex} \hat{D}^\dagger (\alpha) \hat{a} \hat{D}(\alpha) = \hat{a} + \alpha, \label{eqn:13-displacement-property-2} \\
\Rnum{3}&.\hspace{1ex} \hat{D}^\dagger (\alpha) \hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{D}(\alpha) = \hat{a}^\dagger  + \alpha^\star. \label{eqn:13-displacement-property-3}
\end{align}

\callout{Proof of the displacement operator properties.}{%
\begin{proof}
Property \Rnum{1}.
\item For the adjoint of $\hat{D}(\alpha)$, we have:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-displacement-property-1-adjoint-proof}
\begin{split}
    \left(\hat{D}(\alpha)\right)^\dagger &= 
    \left(e^{\alpha \hat{a}^\dagger  - \alpha^\star \hat{a}}\right)^\dagger = \\
    &= e^{\alpha^\star\hat{a}} - \alpha\hat{a}^\dagger \equiv D(-\alpha),
\end{split}
\end{equation}

while for its inverse, suppose $\hat{D}^{-1}(\alpha)$ can be expressed as $e^{x}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-displacement-property-1-inverse-proof}
\begin{split}
    \hat{D}^{-1}(\alpha)\hat{D}(\alpha) \musteq
    \identity \iff x + \alpha \hat{a}^\dagger  - \alpha^\star \hat{a} = 0 \\
    \implies \hat{D}^{-1}(\alpha) = e^{-\alpha \hat{a}^\dagger  + \alpha^\star \hat{a}} \equiv \hat{D}(-\alpha)
\end{split}
\end{equation}

\end{proof}
}
\vspace{1ex}

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Mean photon number of a coherent state}

\hl{A key feature of the coherent state $\ket{\alpha}$ is that it is a right eigenstate of the annihilation operator $\hat{a}$}. This can be proven by applying $\hat{D}^\dagger(\alpha)\hat{a}$ to $\ket{\alpha}$ and using the property \Rnum{2} of the displacement operator (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:13-displacement-property-2}):
\begin{align}
\hat{D}^\dagger (\alpha) \hat{a} \ket{\alpha} &= \hat{D}^\dagger (\alpha) \hat{a} \left(\hat{D}(\alpha) \ket{0}\right) = \nonumber \\[0.5ex]
&= (\hat{a} + \alpha) \ket{0} = 0 + \alpha \ket{0}; \label{eqn:13-proof-eigenvalue-step1}
\end{align}

multiplying both sides from the left by $\hat{D}(\alpha)$, we immediately find:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-annihilation-coherent-eigenvalue}
\hat{a} \ket{\alpha} = \alpha \ket{\alpha}.
\end{equation}

Using this eigenvalue relation, the expectation value for the photon number operator $\hat{n} = \hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a}$ in a coherent state (i.e. the mean number of photons) is easily evaluated:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-mean-photons}
\braket{\alpha|\hat{n}|\alpha} = \braket{\alpha|\hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a}|\alpha} = \alpha^\star \alpha \braket{\alpha|\alpha} = |\alpha|^2 \coloneqq \overline{n}.
\end{equation}

Here, $\overline{n}$ represents the mean photon number, matching the energy intensity of a classical field. However, unlike a number state, \hl{a coherent state does not contain an unequivocal number of photons, since it is composed of an infinite superposition of Fock states}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-state-fock-expansion}
\ket{\alpha} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \braket{n|\alpha} \ket{n}.
\end{equation}

By projecting the eigenvalue equation onto $\bra{n}$ and using the property $\bra{n}\hat{a} = \sqrt{n+1}\bra{n+1}$, one finds the recurrence relation that yields the expansion coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-coefficients}
\braket{n|\alpha} = \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2} \quad \implies \quad \ket{\alpha} = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} \ket{n}.
\end{equation}

\callout{Derivation of coherent state's expansion on number states}{%
We already know that coherent states are eigenstates of the annihilation operator $\hat{a}$; the problem is to find the expansion coefficients $\braket{n|\alpha} \coloneqq c_n$ that satisfy the eigenvalue equation (Eqn.~\ref{eqn:13-annihilation-coherent-eigenvalue}).
\begin{proof}
Let's start by re-writing the eigenvalue equation in terms of the number state basis:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-number-basis-1}
\hat{a}\ket{\alpha} = \hat{a} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n \ket{n} \musteq \alpha \ket{\alpha} 
\end{equation}

$\hat{a}$ slides in the summation and acts on the number state as $\hat{a}\ket{n} = \sqrt{n}\ket{n-1}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-number-basis-2}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n \sqrt{n} \ket{n-1} \musteq \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha\, c_n \ket{n}
\end{equation}

since there does not exist a number state with negative index, we can re-index the left-hand side:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-number-basis-3}
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n+1} \sqrt{n+1} \ket{n} \musteq \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha\, c_n \ket{n}
\end{equation}

this leads to the recurrence relation for the coefficients:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-recurrence}
c_{n+1} = \frac{\alpha\, c_n}{\sqrt{n+1}}.
\end{equation}

Iterating backwards this relation starting from $c_{n+1}$ gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-recurrence-iteration}
\begin{split}
    c_{n+1} &= \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n+1}} 
    \frac{\alpha\, c_{n-1}}{\sqrt{n}} = \\[0.5ex]
    &= \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n+1}} \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n}} 
    \frac{\alpha\, c_{n-1}}{\sqrt{n-1}} = \\[0.5ex]
    &= \dots = \frac{\alpha^{n+1}}{\sqrt{(n+1)!}} c_0
\end{split}
\end{equation}

we can then write the coherent state expansion as:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-with-coefficients}
\ket{\alpha} = c_0 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} \ket{n}.
\end{equation}

$c_0$ is determined applying the normalization condition, keeping in mind that number states are orthonormal ($\braket{n|m} = \delta_{nm}$):
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-normalization}
\braket{\alpha|\alpha} = 
|c_0|^2 \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!} =
|c_0|^2 e^{|\alpha|^2}
\musteq 1
\iff
c_0 = \sqrt{e^{-|\alpha|^2}}
\end{equation}

finally, putting everything together, we obtain the coherent state expansion on the number state basis:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-expansion-final}
\ket{\alpha} = 
e^{-\frac12 |\alpha|^2} 
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} \ket{n}.
\end{equation}
\end{proof}
}

Indeed, the probability $P(n)$ of detecting exactly $n$ photons in a coherent state field matches the photon number distribution:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-poisson-distribution}
P(n) = |c_n|^2 = |\braket{n|\alpha}|^2 = 
\left| 
    e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2} \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}
\right|^2 =
e^{-|\alpha|^2} \frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!} =
e^{-\overline{n}}\, \frac{\overline{n}^n}{n!}.
\end{equation}

where we used the result of Equation~\ref{eqn:13-coherent-mean-photons} ($|\alpha|^2 = \overline{n}$).
 
\calloutinfo{%
    This is exactly the form of a Poisson distribution with mean $\overline{n}$, confirming that the photon statistics of a coherent state is Poissonian. \textbf{The shot-noise in optical detection is a result of quantum nature of light}
}

\subsubsection{Photon number variance of a coherent state}

To compute the variance of the photon number, $(\Delta n)^2 = \braket{\hat{n}^2} - \overline{n}^2$, we expand the operator $\hat{n}^2$ using the commutation relation $[\hat{a}, \hat{a}^\dagger ] = 1$ to put it into a \emph{normal-ordered} form:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-normal-ordering-n2}
\hat{n}^2 = 
\hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a} \hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a} = 
\hat{a}^\dagger  
\left( \hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a} + 1 \right) \hat{a} =
\hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} \hat{a} + \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}.
\end{equation}

Taking the expectation value with respect to $\ket{\alpha}$ yields:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-n2-expectation}
\braket{\alpha|\hat{n}^2|\alpha} = \braket{\alpha|\hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a} \hat{a}|\alpha} + \braket{\alpha|\hat{a}^\dagger  \hat{a}|\alpha} = |\alpha|^4 + |\alpha|^2 = \overline{n}^2 + \overline{n}.
\end{equation}

Substituting this back into the variance definition, we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-photon-variance-final}
(\Delta n)^2 = (\overline{n}^2 + \overline{n}) - \overline{n}^2 = \overline{n}.
\end{equation}

\hl{This identity, where the variance equals the mean $\bigl( (\Delta n)^2 = \overline{n} \bigr)$, is another signature of a quantum \textbf{Poissonian process}}.

This confirms that photocounting statistics of ideal laser light are fully governed by a Poisson distribution with mean intensity $\overline{n}$.

The fractional uncertainty in the number of photons in the coherent state is:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-photon-fractional-uncertainty}
\frac{\Delta n}{\overline{n}} = \frac{\sqrt{\overline{n}}}{\overline{n}} =\frac{1}{\sqrt{\overline{n}}}
\end{equation}
as a matter of course, it decreases with increase in the number mean photon $\overline{n}$.

\calloutwarn{%
It is important to note that \textbf{distinct coherent states are not orthogonal}, as they are projections of shifted wave-packets that overlap in phase space:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-overlap}
\braket{\alpha_1|\alpha_2} = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_1|^2 - \frac{1}{2}|\alpha_2|^2} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(\alpha_1^\star \alpha_2)^n}{n!} = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha_1|^2 - \frac{1}{2}|\alpha_2|^2 + \alpha_1^\star \alpha_2}.
\end{equation}

The physical overlap probability (the profile indistinguishability) is given by the absolute square:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-overlap-squared}
|\braket{\alpha_1|\alpha_2}|^2 = e^{-|\alpha_1 - \alpha_2|^2}.
\end{equation}

Hence, coherent states are only approximately orthogonal when their complex amplitudes are significantly different, i.e., when $|\alpha_1 - \alpha_2| \gg 1$.
}

\calloutinfo{%
The coherent states $\ket{\alpha}$ are quantum states close to the classical ones:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The expectation value of the $\vec{E}$ has the form of a classical field;
    \item The fluctuations in the $\vec{E}$ variables are the same as for the vacuum;
    \item The fluctuations in the fractional uncertainty decreases with increasing the average photon number. This lead us to classical limit where variance of a stable wave vanishes.
    \item The states become well localized in phase with increasing of photon number.
\end{enumerate}
}

If we use:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-quadrature-operators-squares}
\begin{cases}
    \hat{x}_1^2 = \frac{1}{4}
    \left(
        \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger+2\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}+\hat{a}\hat{a}+1
    \right)\\[1ex]
    \hat{x}_2^2 = \frac{1}{4}
    \left(
        -\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a}^\dagger+2\hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a}-\hat{a}\hat{a}+1
    \right)
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

It can be proven that:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-quadrature-variances}
(\Delta \hat{x}_1)^2 = (\Delta \hat{x}_2)^2 = \frac{1}{4}
\end{equation}

\callout{Derivation of the quadrature variances for a coherent state.}{%
\begin{proof}
Let's compute the variance for $\hat{x}_1$, remembering that its definition is found in Equation \ref{eqn:11-quadrature-operators}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-x1-variance-proof}
\begin{split}
(\Delta\hat{x_1})^2 &= 
\braket{\hat{x}_1^2} - \braket{\hat{x}_1}^2 = \\

    \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger )\\[0.5ex]

\end{equation}

we shall write:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-alpha-polar-form}
    \alpha = |\alpha|\, e^{i\theta}
\end{equation}

\end{equation}

\subsection{Time evolution of coherent states}

To understand how coherent states bridge the gap between quantum mechanics and classical electromagnetic theory, we analyze their time evolution in a non-interacting field. We begin by recalling the relationship between the electric field operator $\hat{E}_x$ and the primary quadrature operator $\hat{x}_1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-electric-field-operator-x1-quadrature}
\hat{E}_x = 2A_0\,\hat{x}_1\, \sin(kz), \qquad \text{where } \hat{x}_1 = \frac{1}{2}(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^\dagger).
\end{equation}

Under free time evolution, the complex amplitude $\alpha$ of a coherent state picks up a time-dependent phase factor governed by the mode frequency $\omega$, evolving according to $\ket{\alpha} \to \ket{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}}$. Assuming $\alpha$ is initially real for simplicity, the \hl{expectation values of the quadrature operators evolve as}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-time-evolution}
\begin{split}
    \bra{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} \hat{x}_1 \ket{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} &= \alpha\,  \cos(\omega t), \\[0.3ex]
    \bra{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} \hat{x}_2 \ket{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} &= -\alpha\,  \sin(\omega t).
\end{split}
\end{equation}

In the phasor diagram, this dynamics corresponds to a rigid, clockwise rotation of the uncertainty circle around the origin at a constant angular frequency $\omega$.
\begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images_in_pdf/13-/Grafico_72.pdf}
\end{figure}

By evaluating the expectation value of the electric field operator with respect to this time-evolving state, we map out its macroscopic behavior:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-coherent-field-time-evolution}
\bra{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} \hat{E}_x \ket{\alpha e^{-i\omega t}} = 2A_0\, \alpha \, \sin(kz) \, \cos(\omega t).
\end{equation}

Physically, \hl{the time-dependent variation of the field is determined by the continuous projection of the rotating quadrature $\braket{\hat{x}_1(t)}$ along the real axis}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images_in_pdf/13-/Grafico_73.pdf}
\end{figure}

Equation~\ref{eqn:13-coherent-field-time-evolution} demonstrates that the quantum expectation value perfectly reproduces a classical, monochromatic standing wave pulsing at frequency $\omega$. Notice that the larger the field amplitude ($\overline{n} = |\alpha|^2 \gg 1$), the more classical the system appears. This is because the quantum noise floor (the radius of the uncertainty disk, which is always $1/2$) becomes completely negligible compared to the macroscopic length of the displacement vector $|\alpha|$. 

In this sense, the coherent state is the most classical of all quantum states. This behavior stands in sharp contrast to a photon number state, where the average electric field is identically zero ($\braket{n|\hat{E}_x|n} = 0$) for any $n$, meaning no classical wave oscillations can ever be recovered. 

\calloutinfo{%
    However, while coherent states minimize the uncertainty product using perfectly symmetric, circular fluctuations ($\Delta x_1 = \Delta x_2 = 1/2$), there exist more general minimum-uncertainty states where the noise in one specific quadrature is squeezed below this vacuum limit at the expense of increased noise in the orthogonal variable. These highly non-classical states are known as \textbf{SQUEEZED STATES}.
}

\subsection{Squeezed states}

We have other minimum uncertainty states with: 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-squeezed-condition-uncertainty}
\Delta x_1^2 < \frac{1}{4} \hspace{1cm} \text{or} \hspace{1cm} \Delta x_2^2 < \frac{1}{4}
\end{equation}

This means that we have less noise in one of the quadrature operators, the circle is \textbf{squeezed} into an ellipse of the same area: 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images_in_pdf/13-/Grafico_74.pdf}
\end{figure}

While coherent state have poissonian photon number distribution and shot-noise, \hl{the \textbf{amplitude-squeezed} light will have \textbf{sub-poissonian statistics} with a noise level below the shot-noise.}

These states are represented by 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-squeezed-state-definition}
\ket{\alpha,\epsilon} \quad \text{with} \quad
 \epsilon = r_s \, e^{i2\theta_s},
\end{equation}

where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $|\alpha|^2$ is the intensity of the state.
    \item $\theta_s$ is the orientation of the squeezing axis.
    \item$r_s$ is the degree of squeezing, $\epsilon = 0$ for a coherent state.
\end{itemize}

We can generate a squeezed state starting from a vacuum state:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-squeezed-state-generation}
\ket{\alpha,\epsilon} = \hat{D}(\alpha) \, \hat{S}(\epsilon) \, \ket{0}
\end{equation}

Where we defined the \textbf{squeezing operator}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-squeezing-operator}
    \hat{S}(\epsilon) = 
    \exp{
    \left(
        \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^\star\,\hat{a}^2 -
        \frac{1}{2}\epsilon\,\hat{a}^\dagger 
    \right)}
\end{equation}

We can compute:
\begin{align}\label{eqn:13-squeezed-state-expectation-values}
    \Rnum{1}&.\hspace{1ex}
    \bra{\alpha,\epsilon} \hat{a} \ket{\alpha,\epsilon} = \alpha\\[0.5ex]
    \Rnum{2}&.\hspace{1ex}
    \bra{\alpha,\epsilon} \hat{a}^2 \ket{\alpha,\epsilon} = \alpha^2-\cosh(r_s)\, \sinh(r_s)\, e^{2i\theta_s}\\[0.5ex]
    \Rnum{3}&.\hspace{1ex}
    \bra{\alpha,\epsilon} \hat{a}^\dagger \hat{a} \ket{\alpha,\epsilon} = |\alpha|^2+\sinh^2(r_s)
\end{align}

\calloutinfo{%
    The coherent state is obtained in the limit $r_s \to 0$, while the vacuum state is obtained for $\alpha \to 0$.
}

We can define the \textbf{rotated quadrature operators} for vacuum state:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-rotated-quadrature-operator-squeezed}
\begin{pmatrix}
    \hat{y}_1\\[0.5ex]
    \hat{y_2}
\end{pmatrix} = 
\begin{pmatrix}
    \cos (\tfrac{\theta}{2}) &\sin(\tfrac{\theta}{2})\\[0.5ex]
    -\sin(\tfrac{\theta}{2}) &\cos(\tfrac{\theta}{2})
\end{pmatrix} 
\, 
\begin{pmatrix}
    \hat{x}_1\\[0.5ex]
    \hat{x}_2
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

Or, using Euler's formula:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-rotated-quadrature-operator-squeezed-euler}
\hat{y}_1+i\hat{y}_2 = (\hat{x}_1+\hat{x}_2)\, e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}
\end{equation}

The variances should be:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-squeezed-state-rotated-quadrature-variances}
\begin{cases}
    \Delta \hat{y}_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}e^{-2r_s}\\[0.6ex]
    \Delta \hat{y}_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}e^{+2r_s}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images_in_pdf/13-/Grafico_75.pdf}
\end{figure}

For a generic squeezed state $\ket{\alpha,\epsilon}$, we have:
\[\braket{\hat{y}_1+i\hat{y}_2} = \alpha e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}\]
\begin{figure}[H]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{images_in_pdf/13-/Grafico_76.pdf}
\end{figure}

Error ellipse is the same as for the squeezed vacuum, although displaced by $\alpha$. If we consider the single mode $\hat{E}$:
\[
\hat{E}(t) = A_0 \sin(kz)\bigl(\hat{a}e^{-i\omega t}+\hat{a}^\dagger e^{i\omega t}\bigr)
\]

Where: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\omega t = $ phase of the field.
    \item $\hat{a},\hat{a}^\dagger =$ operators at $t=0$. 
\end{itemize}

If we set $x = \omega t$ and look at the Equation~\ref{eqn:11-electric-field-quadrature-operators}:
\begin{equation*}
\hat{E}(x)= 2A_0\, \sin(kz) [\hat{x}_1\, \cos(x) + \hat{x}_2 \, \sin(x)]
\end{equation*}

From the fact that:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-commutation-relation-quadratures}
\bigl[\hat{x}_1,\hat{x}_2\bigr] = \frac{i}{2}
\end{equation}

 we obtain:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-commutation-relation-electric-field}
\left[\hat{E}(x=0) ,\hat{E}(x)\right] = i A_0^2 \, \sin^2(kz)\, \sin(x)
\end{equation}

\hl{Operators at different phases don't commute!}

\callout{Proof of the commutation relation between $\hat{E}(x=0)$ and $\hat{E}(x)$.}{%
\begin{proof}
Let's start by evaluating the field at $x=0$:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-electric-field-x0}
\hat{E}(x=0) = 2 A_0 \sin(kz)\, \hat{x}_1
\end{equation}

Next, we compute the actual commutator:
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:13-electric-field-commutator-proof}
\begin{split}
\left[
    \hat{E}(0), \hat{E}(x)
\right] &= 
\left(2 A_0\, \sin(kz)\right)^2 
\bigl[
    \hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_1 \cos(x) + \hat{x}_2 \sin(x)
\bigr] = \\[0.5ex]
&= 4 A_0^2 \, \sin^2(kz)
\bigl[
    \hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2 \sin(x)
\bigr] = \\[0.5ex]
&= 4 A_0^2 \, \sin^2(kz) \sin(x) \bigl[
    \hat{x}_1, \hat{x}_2
\bigr]
\end{split}
\end{equation}

where we have used the fact that $\hat{x}_1$ commutes with itself and that $\sin(x)$ is a scalar factor. Substituting the commutation relation in Eqn.~\ref{eqn:13-commutation-relation-quadratures} into the above expression, the result is proven.
\end{proof}
}

If the uncertainty in the field at $x=0$ is:
\[\Delta \hat{E}(0) < \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A_0\,|\sin(kz)\, \sin(x)|\]
Then at some other phases we must have:
\[\Delta \hat{E}(x) > \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} A_0\, |\sin(kz)\, \sin(x)|\]

\calloutinfo{%
    Squeeze light contains phase-dependent noise that is reduced below that of the vacuum for some phases and enhanced above that of the vacuum for others.
}
